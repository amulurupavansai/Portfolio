I joined a global payroll technology company in June 2026 as a Management Trainee on the product track. I came from an electrical engineering background with no payroll experience, so the first months were mostly about learning how to learn. These are the lessons that have stuck. Everything here is general; it is about habits, not about any particular client or product.

## 1. Payroll is a domain where details are the product

A payslip looks simple, but behind every line is a rule: a statutory threshold, a country-specific calculation, a cut-off date. Small differences in how a rule is read can change real people's pay. That makes precision a feature. I now write down the exact rule and its source before discussing a requirement, and I ask "what happens at the edge?" early.

## 2. A good template beats a perfect document

When you need to produce many similar documents, the first one matters most. I spent extra time getting the structure of the first requirements document right and then reused it as the template. Reviewers learned one format, and the rest of the work moved faster. Consistency also makes gaps easier to spot.

## 3. Use AI as a first draft, never as the last word

Generative AI is useful for condensing long workshop transcripts or comparing documents. It is not a source of truth. My routine is simple: let the tool produce a digest, check it against the source, keep a list of gaps and open questions, and review that list with the team. The tool saves time; the review is what makes the result trustworthy.

## 4. Testing AI features is a product skill

Evaluating an AI assistant is different from checking a normal feature, because the same question can produce different answers. What helped me was writing realistic scenarios, agreeing on what a good answer looks like before running anything, and recording the exact inputs so results can be reproduced. Clear criteria turn opinions into evidence.

## 5. Ask for the "why" behind manual work

Whenever something is done by hand, there is usually an undocumented set of rules behind it. Working backwards from the output to the logic is slow but valuable, and writing the assumptions down lets engineers pick it up without guessing. Automation starts with understanding.

## 6. Be the clear point of contact

Work that crosses teams loses information at every handoff. Sending a short written summary after a conversation, naming owners and next steps, prevents a surprising amount of rework. Being reliable matters more than being fast.

## 7. Choose what not to automate yet

Moving manual checks to automated ones is attractive, but not every flow is ready. Stable, high-value, frequently repeated flows are the best starting point, and the people who own a module should be involved from day one. Deciding what to leave for later is part of the job.

## What's next

I want to keep building depth in requirements, quality and AI evaluation, and learn how product decisions are made end to end. If you work in HR-tech or payroll and have advice for someone early in their product career, I would be glad to hear it.
